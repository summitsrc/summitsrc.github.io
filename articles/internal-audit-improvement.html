7 Signs Your Internal Audit Programme Is Not Driving Improvement

An audit calendar can be complete while the management system continues to repeat the same failures. These signs help distinguish activity from effective assurance.

Internal auditing should provide more than completed checklists and closed schedules. Its value comes from whether the programme identifies meaningful weaknesses, tests whether controls actually work and helps management make better decisions. When the same issues recur, findings remain superficial or audits simply confirm that documents exist, the programme may be active without being effective.

1. The same findings keep returning

Recurring findings often indicate that corrective actions addressed the symptom rather than the cause. A reminder, toolbox talk or retraining session may be useful as a correction, but it is not automatically a sustainable corrective action.

2. Audits focus on documents rather than process performance

A procedure can be perfectly written while the process is poorly controlled. Effective auditors compare requirements with what is actually happening through observation, records, interviews and system data.

3. Findings are vague

Statements such as “records need improvement” are difficult to defend and difficult to correct. A strong finding identifies the requirement, the evidence sampled and the specific gap.

4. High-risk processes receive the same attention as low-risk areas

Audit programmes should consider process importance, changes, previous results and organisational risk. Equal frequency does not always produce equal assurance.

5. Corrective actions are closed without effectiveness evidence

Closure should demonstrate more than completion. The organisation should be able to show that the action reduced or eliminated recurrence over an appropriate period.

6. Management receives results but does not use them

Audit outputs should inform decisions about resources, process controls, competence, risk and improvement. Reports that are filed but not discussed create little organisational value.

7. Auditors do not follow evidence trails

The most useful audit evidence often appears when the auditor follows a transaction, record, work order or incident through the process. Strictly following a prepared checklist can prevent the team from exploring meaningful inconsistencies.

Summit takeaway: A strong audit programme measures effectiveness by the quality of assurance and improvement it creates, not by the number of audits completed.
