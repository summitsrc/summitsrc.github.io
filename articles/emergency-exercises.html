Tabletop Exercises Should Test Decisions, Not Just Procedures

The strongest exercises reveal how people actually communicate, escalate, isolate hazards and coordinate response under pressure.

Emergency exercises are sometimes treated as demonstrations of a procedure. Participants are told what should happen, the discussion follows the written sequence and the exercise ends with very few findings. That may build familiarity, but it does not provide strong assurance.

Start with an objective

Define exactly what the exercise is intended to test. For example, notification and escalation, casualty management, hazardous-material identification, tactical command, external responder mobilisation or continuity of critical operations.

Separate strategic and tactical response

Management teams make strategic decisions about escalation, communications, business impact and recovery. Tactical responders make immediate decisions about isolation, PPE, access, evacuation, containment and handover. Both perspectives matter, but exercises should be clear about which level is being tested.

Use injects that force decisions

Provide changing information such as an injured worker, uncertain substance identification, wind direction, failed communications or delayed external support. The purpose is not to trick participants. It is to expose assumptions and test whether response arrangements are resilient.

Observe information flow

Many response failures are not caused by lack of effort but by incomplete information. Evaluators should note who knew what, when they knew it and whether critical details reached the right role in time.

End with accountable improvement

Every meaningful gap should become an action with an owner and target date. Where appropriate, actions should be tracked through the organisation's corrective-action or work-management process and verified for effectiveness.

Summit takeaway: A successful exercise is not one with no findings. It is one that discovers weaknesses safely, before a real emergency does.
